Planning doesn't need a system, a notebook, or an evening ritual. Here's the whole method: **once a day, say everything on your mind out loud, pick the three things that actually matter, and put the list where you'll see it all day.** Two minutes. If you do it while the coffee brews, it costs you nothing.

People who hate planning hate it because most planning advice is really *organizing* advice — projects, tags, hierarchies, weekly templates. You don't need any of that. You need tomorrow's list to exist and to be visible. Everything below is in service of those two things.

## Step 1: Empty your head (45 seconds)

The goal of the first minute is to stop holding tasks in your memory, because working memory is tiny and unreliable — every task you're "keeping in mind" is leaking attention from whatever you're actually doing.

So: just talk. Don't organize while you speak, don't decide what's important yet, don't split big things into small things. Dump.

> "Okay — dentist tomorrow, the report is due Friday, mom's birthday is next week, I need to buy a gift, package pickup closes at six, and I keep meaning to fix the kitchen drawer."

That's six tasks in fifteen seconds. Speaking is faster than typing by roughly a factor of three, and more importantly it doesn't require your eyes or hands — you can do it while doing something else.

Two rules make the dump work:

- **Include the vague ones.** "Sort out the birthday" is a fine thing to say now; deciding *what* the plan is can wait. Vague tasks captured beat precise tasks forgotten.
- **Include today's leftovers.** The stuff you didn't finish yesterday goes back on the pile now, not at 11 p.m. when you're trying to sleep.

If you use a voice-first to-do app, all of this lands as dated items without further effort — that's exactly what they're for. If you prefer Apple's Reminders, a single "today" list with a brain-dump entry per line does fine; you can sort it out in step 2.

## Step 2: Pick three (45 seconds)

Now the only real decision of the morning: **which three things, if they get done, would make today a win?**

Three is not a magic incantation — it's a forcing function. A list of twelve "priorities" is a list with zero priorities, and it will be noon before you stop deciding what to start. Three fits in your head, fits on a Lock Screen, and forces the tradeoff that planning exists to make.

Move everything else off today:

- Things that can wait → give them a real date later this week. "Someday" means never.
- The vague ones → leave them undated for now; they'll get resolved in the weekly sweep, not in your two-minute morning.
- Genuine emergencies → fine, they bump something off the three. Swap, don't add.

One caveat worth saying plainly: three is the target for *main* work, not the totality of your day. Dentist appointments and package pickups happen whether or not they're top three — the list isn't a contract, it's a default answer to "what now?" for when the day gets loud.

## Step 3: Park it where you'll see it (30 seconds)

A plan in an app you won't open is a plan you won't follow. The last step is placement.

Put the three (plus today's fixed appointments) on your **Lock Screen** — as a Lock Screen widget if your to-do app has one, or failing that, pinned to the top of a Reminders widget on your first Home Screen page. The standard to aim for: every time you raise your phone, the day's plan is the first thing your eyes land on. You should not be able to check the weather without seeing what you said you'd do.

From that point on, your job is not to remember the plan. Your job is to trip over it repeatedly, all day, until it's done.

## Why not plan the night before?

Some people love night-before planning, and if it's working for you, keep it — capturing tomorrow's tasks as they occur to you in the evening is genuinely excellent. But as a *primary* habit it fails for two reasons: at night you're tired and everything feels doable (hello, twelve-item fantasy list), and by morning the plan is stale — the world delivered new information overnight.

The morning dump takes the world as it actually is today. Two minutes of reality beats twenty minutes of last night's fiction.

## The evening that makes mornings easier

There is one evening habit worth keeping, and it takes thirty seconds: before you put the phone down, look at today's list once. Not to judge — just to notice. What didn't get done? Say it into tomorrow's dump then and there ("the report thing slipped again") and let it go. This is also when you'd notice a task you've pushed four days running, which is a task that needs to be broken down or dropped, not re-pushed a fifth time.

That's the whole loop: two minutes in the morning, thirty seconds at night. No review guilt, no system to maintain.

## Appointments vs. tasks: keep them apart

One distinction makes the morning dump much calmer: **appointments are where you have to *be*; tasks are what you have to *do*.** The 2:00 pm dentist visit is an appointment — it lives in your calendar, and it defends its own time slot. "Buy milk" is a task — it needs a list, not a time.

The mistake to avoid is putting tasks *in* your calendar as fake 30-minute blocks ("7:00–7:30, buy milk"). Fake blocks fail the first time traffic is bad, and then you start ignoring your own calendar — which is a much worse loss than a messy to-do list. Put the dentist in the calendar, milk on the list, and let the list sit beside your calendar on the Lock Screen where both are one glance away.

If your to-do app can mirror due items into Apple Calendar (VoiceTodo can, optionally), use it for tasks with a real deadline — "report due Friday" — and keep everything else list-only. The test: does it have to happen *at* a time, or just *by* a time? At → calendar. By → list.

## Weekends and the occasional skipped day

The routine doesn't break when you skip it — it breaks when skipping makes you feel like a failure, because then you quit. Two allowances worth building in:

- **Weekends get a one-item version.** Say one thing that matters ("call mom"), see it on the Lock Screen, done. The habit survives; the stakes drop.
- **A missed morning is a two-o'clock restart.** The dump works at 2 pm exactly as well as at 8 am — say the list then, pick three for what's left of the day. A plan for half a day beats no plan and a dose of guilt.

What you're really training is a reflex, not a schedule: *plan falls out of head → plan lands on Lock Screen*. Reflexes don't care what time it is.
