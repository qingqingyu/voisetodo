You don't forget things because your memory is bad. You forget them because your brain is asked to store them while it's busy doing something else. The fix is two habits, not one: **write it down the second you think of it — in a way that takes zero effort — and put the list somewhere you'll see it without trying.** Miss either half and the system quietly fails.

This guide walks through why that works and how to set it up on an iPhone in about ten minutes.

## Why you forget (it's not your memory)

Psychologists distinguish between working memory — the small scratchpad you use right now — and long-term memory. "Call the dentist tomorrow" is born while your working memory is already juggling a conversation, a commute, or a half-finished email. An intention that only ever lives on that scratchpad gets overwritten within minutes, not because you're careless but because the scratchpad holds roughly four things at once and something else always needs the space.

This is why forgotten tasks feel so unfair: you *did* think of it. You thought of it at the exact right moment, in the dentist's waiting room, with the phone number on the wall in front of you. What was missing was a place for the thought to land that isn't your head.

There's a second, quieter failure mode: you did write it down, three weeks ago, in an app you've since stopped opening. A task list you never look at is the same as no task list. So a working system has two halves, and both have to be easy:

1. **Capture must be effortless** — faster than the thought escaping.
2. **Review must be unavoidable** — the list has to appear in front of you on its own.

## Capture: the two-second rule

A capture method only works if you can use it in the middle of whatever you were doing. Test any method with this question: *can I get the thought out of my head in under two seconds, without breaking stride?*

- **Typing fails for most people.** Unlock, find the app, hit plus, tap the title field, type, save. It's ten seconds of high-attention work, and high-attention work is exactly what you don't have when a task occurs to you. Tasks that take effort to record get recorded never.
- **Voice capture passes easily.** Hold a button, say "call the dentist tomorrow," release. Two seconds, no accuracy needed, no looking at the screen. This is why voice-first to-do apps exist, and why they tend to stick where typing-first apps don't.
- **Siri passes too.** "Hey Siri, remind me to call the dentist tomorrow" works even when your phone is in your pocket or across the room. It's the fastest capture that requires zero hands.

Apple's built-in Reminders app deserves credit here: its capture is genuinely quick, especially through Siri, and if it's already part of your habits there's no reason to switch for capture's sake alone. The more common failure is elsewhere — in the review half.

## Review: put the list where you already look

Here is the uncomfortable question: how many times today did you open your to-do app? Now — how many times did you look at your phone?

For most people the ratio is about one to fifty. Your phone gets looked at constantly; your task app almost never. The list doesn't need a better app, it needs a better *location* — it needs to ride along with something you already do.

Three placements work on an iPhone, in decreasing order of effectiveness:

1. **The Lock Screen.** You raise your phone dozens of times a day; the Lock Screen is the one page you cannot avoid. A Lock Screen widget that shows your next few tasks — checkable right there — turns every unconscious phone-check into a review. This is the single highest-leverage change most people can make.
2. **The first Home Screen page.** A medium or large widget beside your most-used apps gets seen every time you open anything. Good second line of defense, and interactive widgets let you check things off without launching anything.
3. **A recurring reminder to review.** The fallback: a daily alarm at a time you're naturally free (with coffee, at lunch) that says "look at the list." It works, but it costs willpower every single day, which is why it ranks last.

The goal of all three is the same: your list should reach you. A list you have to remember to check is a list you will forget to check — that's just the scratchpad problem wearing a nicer outfit.

## Keep the list honest with a weekly sweep

There's a last failure mode, and it's the one that kills systems after a month or two: the list grows faster than it shrinks. Stale tasks pile up, the list becomes a wall of guilt, and looking at it hurts, so you stop. The forgetting problem comes back disguised as an avoidance problem.

The fix is a short weekly sweep — ten minutes, same time every week. For everything still unfinished, make one of four decisions:

- **Do it this week** — it matters and it's ripe.
- **Push it** — give it a real future date, not "someday". If you've pushed it three weeks running, that's information: it's probably a want, not a task.
- **Break it down** — if it keeps sliding because it's vague ("sort out the insurance"), replace it with the next concrete step ("find the policy PDF").
- **Drop it** — guilt-free. A deleted task costs nothing; a zombie task costs a little attention every single day.

The sweep is what keeps the list short enough to fit on a Lock Screen widget — and a list that fits on your Lock Screen is a list you'll actually see.

## The two-minute audit: find your personal leak

If tasks still slip through, don't blame your memory — audit the pipe. For one typical day, notice where each task went when you thought of it:

- **Never captured at all?** The capture method was too slow for the moment. Notice *what you were doing* when the thought appeared: driving, cooking, mid-meeting. Your capture method has to work in that exact posture, which is why hands-free beats every screen-based method.
- **Captured but never seen?** The review half is broken. Move the list somewhere unavoidable (start with the Lock Screen — it's the cheapest win), and check that the list isn't so long it's become wallpaper.
- **Seen but skipped repeatedly?** The task itself is the problem, not the system. It's either vague (needs splitting into a next step) or a want pretending to be a need (needs dropping, honestly).

Most people discover they don't have a memory problem at all — they have a "captured during a meeting, never looked at again" problem, which is a placement problem, and placement is fixable in an afternoon.
